Losing customers to a cheaper competitor

Foodpanda is cheaper on almost every basket. The useful question is why.

Both apps reveal fees at checkout, both list the same restaurants, both run coupons. So the gap is not a design choice we can copy — it comes from what a delivery costs each company to complete. That cost is set by how many drops a rider finishes in an hour, which is set by how tightly packed the orders are.

What one delivery costs us

Riders are paid per delivery, and a short in-zone trip is a smaller job than a long one. So as density rises, pay per delivery falls while pay per hour rises — and our cost per delivery falls with it. Both numbers below move together.

What this means for strategy

If the gap is structural, the obvious responses are the losing ones.
✕
Match their discounts
Every taka comes out of our margin, while theirs is partly restaurant-funded and partly absorbed by lower cost. Same race, lost faster.
✕
Cover more of the city
Wider coverage spreads the same riders thinner, raising cost per delivery and widening the gap we are trying to close.
1
Shrink the service area, raise density
Two zones served properly instead of eight served badly. Density is the one input we control, and it is what the price gap is made of.
2
Sell certainty instead of cheapness
We cannot promise the lowest price. We can promise one flat fee for the whole zone, published and falling as the zone gets busier — a claim a city-wide competitor cannot easily copy.
3
Restaurant-funded offers
Our lower commission is worth something to a restaurant. Trade it for exclusives and funded discounts, so offers stop coming out of our margin.
4
A membership, because it feeds density
Subscribers order more often inside the same small area, raising drops per rider-hour, which lowers cost, which pays for the membership.

How they actually fit more drops into an hour

Their advantage is not mapping — both companies use the same third-party maps. It is dispatch, and dispatch only works when orders sit close together.
1
The rider is not sent to wait
Their system does not assign the nearest rider. It assigns the rider who will arrive at the restaurant roughly when the food is ready, so nobody stands around for ten minutes per order. Idle time is the hidden cost in every delivery.
2
Two orders, one trip
When timing and location line up, one rider carries several deliveries in a single run. Two orders on one trip halves the cost of each.
!
Neither works without density
You cannot batch two orders that are six kilometres apart, and you cannot time arrivals precisely when every trip is long. Both techniques are available to us — they simply have nothing to work with until orders are packed close. That is why density comes first and the clever dispatch comes second.

What I would verify before committing

  • Orders completed per rider-hour, by zone, for the last quarter — the number the whole argument rests on.
  • A matched-basket comparison: identical restaurant, identical items, all-in totals on both apps, across several zones.
  • The falsifiable test: if density is the cause, the price gap should be wider in our low-density zones and narrower in dense ones. If the gap is flat everywhere, density is not the explanation and this plan should not be built.
  • Who funds the competitor’s coupons — if the same restaurant’s discount appears on both apps, it is restaurant-funded and we can ask for it too.

The fix is a loop, not a feature

Each step lowers the cost of the next one. That is why the order matters, and why cutting coverage — which looks like retreat — is the first move rather than the last.

Discounting attacks the symptom and spends our own margin. Density attacks the cause and pays for itself, which is why it is the only version of “be cheaper” we can actually sustain.

Does the rider just work harder for the same money?

The obvious objection, and the one that would kill this plan if the answer were yes. It is not — but only if the pay model is built correctly from the start.
✕
What we must not do: keep hourly pay flat
Holding rider pay flat while drops rise from 1.6 to 4.0 means the entire saving is taken out of the rider’s effort. That is not a cost improvement, it is a wage cut wearing a disguise.
✓
What we do: pay per delivery, and the trip itself gets shorter
A sparse delivery is 6 km and 35 minutes; an in-zone one is 1.5 km and 15 minutes. Those are genuinely different jobs. At ৳82 for the long trip a rider earns ৳132 an hour. At ৳46 for the short one they earn ৳184 an hour — more money, less fuel, less fatigue.
=
The saving is created, not extracted
Deleted distance and deleted idle time are new value that did not exist before. Rider pay per hour rises about 40% and our cost per delivery still falls from ৳90 to ৳52 — both sides take a share of what density created, and neither pays for the other.
!
The guardrail that enforces it
Rider earnings per hour is tracked as a release guardrail alongside delivery time. If cost per delivery falls while rider hourly earnings do not rise, the plan is being implemented wrongly and stops — regardless of how good the unit economics look.

Why the membership is the engine, not a perk

Foodie Plus is the only part of the plan that feeds the loop back into itself, which is why it ships last and matters most.
৳
৳149 a month, delivery free inside your zone
It saves ৳40 on every order, so it pays for itself on the fourth order of the month. The cart shows that arithmetic against the customer’s own ordering frequency instead of claiming it.
↻
A member orders more often, in the same small area
That is the point. Each extra order lands inside the two zones we already serve, so it adds density rather than spreading us thinner — and density is what lowers cost per delivery.
=
The lower cost is what pays for the free delivery
We are not giving delivery away out of margin. Members create the density that makes delivery cheap, and the saving is handed back to them. That loop is why a competitor cannot simply copy the price.
!
It fails if members do not reorder
Below four orders a month, a member costs us more than they bring. That is the stop condition in Phase 3, and it is why the membership launches only after Phase 1 proves cost per delivery actually fell.
